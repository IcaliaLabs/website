The first number most teams compare is the hourly rate. It's also the least useful one. What matters is how much you pay for each **productive** engineer on your team — and that depends as much on hiring speed, ramp-up, and retention as on the rate itself.

## The short answer

Senior nearshore engineers from Mexico and Latin America usually cost substantially less than equivalent US hires, while working in your time zone as part of your team. For example, [Everlance](/case-studies.html), a San Francisco-based startup, added eight senior engineers across backend and native mobile — same-quality hires at **60% cost savings** — and every engineer stayed through the company's acquisition.

## The costs that don't show up on the invoice

### 1. The empty seat

Every month a role stays open is a month of roadmap that doesn't ship. Senior hires in the US can take months to close [VERIFY: cite source]. A partner with a vetted network can present matched candidates in days.

### 2. Ramp-up

New engineers aren't productive on day one. The faster someone learns your codebase and conventions, the sooner they pay for themselves. Engineers who join your standups, tools, and code review from the first day ramp up fastest.

### 3. Turnover

Turnover is the biggest hidden cost in engineering. When an engineer leaves, you pay recruiting and ramp-up again, and you lose their context. A lower rate with high churn is almost never the cheaper option. At Icalia Labs, engineers average five-plus years of tenure.

### 4. Management and coordination

Teams that share your working hours need less process to stay aligned. Teams that don't need more written specs, more handoffs, and more senior time spent coordinating — a real cost even if nobody invoices for it.

## What a staff augmentation rate covers

A staff augmentation rate looks higher than a salary because it includes everything around the engineer:

- Sourcing and technical vetting
- Mandatory employment costs and benefits in the engineer's country
- Equipment and tooling
- An account manager and delivery support
- A replacement pipeline if an engineer isn't the right fit

When you compare it with hiring in-house, compare it with the *fully loaded* in-house cost plus recruiting fees — not the base salary.

## A simple way to compare options

For each option, estimate over 12 months:

1. **Rate or fully loaded salary**, times the months you'll actually have the engineer.
2. **Months the seat is empty** before someone starts.
3. **Ramp-up months** at reduced productivity.
4. **Expected turnover**, times the cost of re-hiring and re-ramping.
5. **Coordination overhead** from time-zone gaps.

The option with the lowest total — not the lowest rate — is your cheapest engineer.
