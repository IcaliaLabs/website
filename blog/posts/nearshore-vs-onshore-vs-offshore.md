If you're scaling an engineering team in the US, you have three sourcing models: hire **onshore** (in the US), go **nearshore** (Mexico and Latin America), or go **offshore** (Asia, Eastern Europe, and beyond). Each one trades cost against collaboration speed differently. This guide compares them on the factors that actually decide whether a hire works out.

## The short answer

For product teams that ship continuously — where engineers need to be in standups, review each other's code the same day, and respond to incidents live — **nearshore is usually the best balance**. You keep real-time collaboration, get access to a deep pool of senior engineers, and pay less than an equivalent US hire.

Onshore is still right when you need someone physically on-site or when the role has strict residency requirements. Offshore works for clearly scoped, low-interaction work where async handoffs are fine.

## Time-zone overlap is the factor teams underestimate

Hourly rates are easy to compare. The cost of waiting a day for an answer isn't.

When your engineers share your working hours, a blocked pull request gets unblocked in minutes, a production incident gets a second pair of eyes immediately, and product decisions happen in a conversation instead of a thread. When they don't, every question becomes a 12-to-24-hour round trip.

Monterrey, where Icalia Labs is headquartered, runs on **Central Standard Time year-round**. That gives a full shared workday with US Central and Mountain teams and most of the day with Eastern and Pacific teams.

## Cost: compare total cost, not hourly rate

The fair comparison is **total cost per productive engineer**, not the rate on an invoice. Count:

- **Recruiting cost and time.** Every month a seat stays open is a month of roadmap you don't ship.
- **Ramp-up.** An engineer who needs three months to become productive costs you three months of salary before they pay off.
- **Retention.** Turnover resets ramp-up and recruiting. A cheaper engineer who leaves in nine months is rarely cheaper.
- **Management overhead.** Async-only teams need more written specs, more coordination, and more senior oversight.

## Quality depends on who you hire, not where they live

Senior engineers exist in every market. What differs is how hard they are to find and how well a vendor vets them. At Icalia Labs, fewer than 0.4% of applicants are accepted, and engineers average five-plus years with us — which is why clients like [Everlance kept every engineer through its acquisition](/case-studies.html).

Ask any partner — nearshore or offshore — for their acceptance rate, average tenure, and how they replace an engineer who isn't working out.

## When each model is the right call

**Choose onshore when:**

- The role requires being on-site, a security clearance, or US residency.
- You're hiring a small number of leaders who will define team culture.

**Choose nearshore when:**

- Engineers need to work inside your team day to day — standups, pairing, code review, on-call.
- You need senior capacity in weeks, not quarters.
- You work in a regulated domain like [fintech](/industries/fintech.html) or [healthtech](/industries/healthtech.html) and need people who can be onboarded into your compliance processes.

**Choose offshore when:**

- The work is well-specified and can tolerate async handoffs.
- Lowest hourly cost matters more than collaboration speed.

## How to evaluate a nearshore partner

1. **Seniority.** Ask for average years of experience and how they verify it.
2. **Vetting.** Ask what share of applicants they accept and what the technical assessment covers.
3. **Integration.** Engineers should join *your* rituals and tools, not work through a project manager.
4. **Retention and replacement.** Ask for average tenure and how fast they replace an engineer.
5. **Proof.** Ask for named case studies with measurable outcomes.
